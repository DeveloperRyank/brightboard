# Standard English Conventions

Four question types make up this module. Each hands you a sentence with a blank and asks you to pick the mark, verb, or phrase that fits. The habit that moves the score is to stop trusting your ear. These items are decided by structure, not by sound. A sentence can read smoothly and still be a run-on; a verb you would never say aloud can still be right. Work the parts, not the vibe.

## Sentence boundaries and punctuation  <!-- concept: sentence_boundaries -->

- **What the item really tests.** Whether you can find the main verb, decide if each side of the blank is a complete thought, and pick a mark strong enough for the relation between them. Wrong options put too strong a mark between inseparable pieces, too weak a mark between independent ones, or a mark inside a single clause.

- **The method.**
  1. Cover the blank. Read the left chunk alone, then the right chunk alone. Is each a complete sentence?
  2. Label each side INDEP or DEP. A chunk opening with although, because, since, when, while, which, that, or unless is dependent no matter how finished it sounds.
  3. Two INDEPs need a strong link: period, semicolon, colon or dash, or a comma plus a FANBOYS word (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). A bare comma between them is a splice.
  4. INDEP plus DEP needs a light link, usually a comma or nothing; never a period or semicolon, which strands the DEP side as a fragment.
  5. Before a colon or dash the front must be INDEP; after it, the back may be a clause, phrase, or list restating the front.
  6. Refuse any mark between subject and verb, verb and object, or a noun and its defining that, who, or which clause.

- **Worked example.** The curator dated every portrait in the east wing ____ the earliest turned out to be a 1611 self-study.
  A) ,
  B) ;
  C) which
  D) because of
  Key is B. Both sides are independent clauses and the second explains the first, so a semicolon carries the join with no conjunction.

- **Worked example.** The lab's revised protocol had three features ____ shorter cycles, cheaper reagents, and a 4 a.m. handoff.
  A) ,
  B) ;
  C) :
  D) and
  Key is C. The front is complete and the back is a list naming those features, which is what a colon is for. A semicolon wants a sentence after it; a comma cannot open a list after an unbroken predicate.

- **The traps.**
  Comma splice. Both sides stand alone but only a comma joins them. Tell: two subjects, two finite verbs, no FANBOYS word.
  Fragment join. A period or semicolon right before a chunk starting with because, although, which, or when. Tell: read that chunk alone and it hangs.
  Forbidden split. A mark wedged inside one clause, usually between a noun and its that-clause or between a verb and its object. Tell: no option you pick makes both sides a sentence.
  Colon misfire. A colon after an incomplete front, or one used when the back continues the predicate instead of explaining it. Tell: the front's verb is still looking for its object.

- **Timing tactic.** Thirty to forty seconds. Read the two chunks twice, not the whole sentence four times. Compare the options vertically: three pure-punctuation choices mean you are deciding strength; an option adding which or because means you are also deciding independence.

- **Language to use.** "Can both sides stand alone?" "Strong mark for a strong relation." "Never cut inside a clause."

## Clauses, fragments and run-ons  <!-- concept: clause_structure -->

- **What the item really tests.** Whether the finished sentence still has one finite main verb for its main subject once every subordinator and -ing form is accounted for. The blank usually offers four shapes of the same predicate, and only one supplies the verb the sentence lacks.

- **The method.**
  1. Underline every verb-shaped word, including -ing forms, infinitives, and participles.
  2. Cross out every verb inside a clause beginning with that, which, who, because, although, if, when, since, or while. Those verbs are borrowed by the subordinator.
  3. If nothing survives, the sentence has a hole; the right option supplies the finite verb and, if needed, the subject.
  4. Watch for the strander: a comma plus an -ing form, "..., creating the first ...", with no other finite verb in sight. An -ing form modifies, it never serves as the main verb.
  5. Read the sentence aloud as a standalone statement. A hanging ending means the option made that side dependent.
  6. When one side is already dependent, only an independent clause fits the other. A comma between them is enough; a period or semicolon is not.
  7. Never let two subordinators share a sentence. "Although ... but ..." or "Because ... so ..." leaves neither side able to anchor.

- **Worked example.** Because the archive was closed for renovations, ____ the research team to work from scanned copies only.
  A) forcing
  B) it forced
  C) which forced
  D) having forced
  Key is B. The because-clause cannot be the main clause, so the blank must carry both a subject and a finite verb. The other three leave the sentence with no main verb at all.

- **The traps.**
  Stranded participle. An -ing form after a comma posing as the main verb. Tell: cross out everything from the comma onward and no finite verb remains.
  Subordinator swap. An option adding which, that, who, or because to the side that was supposed to be independent. Tell: both sides now begin with subordinators, or the sentence never reaches a period-worthy end.
  Double subordinator. "Although ... but" or "Since ... so." Tell: two openers, no anchor.
  Helper-less perfect. A bare had or has with no past participle to pair with, or a participle with no auxiliary in front of it.

- **Timing tactic.** Forty-five seconds, because the four options look nearly identical. Do not reread the sentence for each. Scan the options vertically, spot the shape difference (finite verb, participle, subordinator, infinitive), then plug only the two plausible ones.

- **Language to use.** "Find the main verb or the sentence has none." "Subordinators borrow verbs; who gets it back?" "Read it standalone."

## Subject-verb agreement and verb tense  <!-- concept: agreement_and_tense -->

- **What the item really tests.** Two things at once: whether the verb agrees with the true subject, which is usually buried under an interrupter phrase, and whether the verb's time matches the timeline the sentence already states.

- **The method.**
  1. Locate the verb in the blank, then pencil out every prepositional phrase (of the ..., in the ..., along with ...) and anything set off by commas or dashes. What remains is the true subject.
  2. Respect quantifier heads. "Each of the answers is" is singular because the head is Each. In "one of the reports that mention floods," the main verb agrees with one but the that-clause verb agrees with reports.
  3. On either ... or and neither ... nor, the verb agrees with the closer noun: "neither the dean nor the students were," but "neither the students nor the dean was."
  4. Two subjects joined by and take a plural verb unless they name one thing (the founder and CEO was) or unless each or every fronts both (each boy and each girl was).
  5. Collective nouns (team, committee, faculty, herd, staff) are singular when acting as a unit, and the SAT almost always wants the unit reading.
  6. Set the tense anchor. Circle the time words already in the sentence (yesterday, since 2019, by the time they arrived, every spring) and match the option to them.
  7. Past perfect marks only the earlier of two past events; present perfect pairs with since and for; a general fact stays present even inside past narration ("Copernicus argued that the Earth moves around the Sun").

- **Worked example.** Each of the surviving letters from the expedition ____ a different version of the same storm.
  A) describe
  B) describes
  C) describing
  D) have described
  Key is B. Strip the interrupter "of the surviving letters" and the subject is Each, which is singular, so the singular present verb wins.

- **The traps.**
  Attraction. The noun nearest the blank is plural but is not the subject. Tell: it sits inside an of-phrase.
  Interrupter hiding the head. A long set-off phrase makes a singular subject look like the plural noun at its tail. Tell: cover the words between the two commas and reread.
  Tense jump with no cause. A past-anchored sentence paired with a present progressive option. Tell: no time word justifies the shift.
  Wrong since partner. A simple past attached to "since 2015." Tell: since and for want present perfect.
  Missing past perfect. Two finished past events, one clearly earlier, and the earlier stays simple past. Tell: look for before, after, by the time, or already.

- **Timing tactic.** Thirty seconds for agreement, thirty-five for tense. Cover the interrupter so only the subject and the blank are visible; for tense, circle the time words before touching the options.

- **Language to use.** "Strip the interruption, then match." "Nearest is not subject." "Set the time before you tense the verb."

## Pronouns, modifiers and parallel structure  <!-- concept: reference_and_modifier -->

- **What the item really tests.** Whether the noun an opening phrase describes is standing right after it, whether a pronoun points at exactly one antecedent, and whether every item in a series or comparison joined by and, or, than, or a correlative pair shares one grammatical shape.

- **The method.**
  1. If the sentence opens with a phrase before a comma, ask what that phrase describes. The noun right after the blank must be that thing, and must be an actor if the opener implies action.
  2. Apply the same test to openers like "Unlike X," "To prepare for the trial," "Written in 1902," or any -ing or -ed participle.
  3. For a pronoun in the blank (it, they, them, this, that, which, both, their), point at exactly one antecedent and match number. Two possible nouns means the option is wrong.
  4. Distrust a which that reaches back over a whole clause; the SAT wants which to name a specific noun.
  5. Cut the sentence at every and or or and inspect the pieces on both sides. Three nouns, three -ing forms, three that-clauses, three infinitives. Mixed shapes lose.
  6. With correlative pairs (not only ... but also, both ... and, either ... or), the words after the first half must match the words after the second half in shape.
  7. In comparisons with than or as, put the same category on both sides: compare a city to a city, not a city's climate to a city.

- **Worked example.** After reviewing all nineteen complaint files, ____ the tenant's claim.
  A) the complaint was dismissed by the housing board
  B) the housing board dismissed
  C) there was a dismissal of
  D) dismissing was standard for
  Key is B. The opener describes whoever did the reviewing, and only B puts that actor right after the comma. The others dangle, attaching reviewing to a complaint, to there, or to an act.

- **The traps.**
  Dangling opener. The intro phrase implies an actor but the noun after the comma is a thing. Tell: the doer of the -ing word never shows up in subject position.
  Split reference. They, it, or this could point at two antecedents, or a which reaches back over an entire clause instead of one noun. Tell: you can name both targets and the sentence still reads the same, or inserting "which fact" makes it clearer.
  Faulty parallel. An infinitive beside a gerund, or a noun beside a verb phrase in one list. Tell: cut at and and the halves are different kinds of things.
  Comparison mismatch. The two sides of than are not the same category. Tell: the sentence compares a thing to a property of a thing.

- **Timing tactic.** Forty seconds. For modifiers, cover everything up to the first comma and look only at the noun right after it. For parallel items, say the list pieces aloud and listen for the odd shape out.

- **Language to use.** "The opening describes the noun right after it." "One pronoun, one target." "Cut at 'and' and check both halves match."
