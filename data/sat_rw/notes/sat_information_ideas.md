# Information and Ideas

This domain asks what every reader faces at the end of a page: what did I get from that, and what may I say next? Passages are short, one question each, and about half these items hand you a table, chart, or graph. One habit moves the score: state your own version of the answer before your eyes reach the options. Weak performers are not poor readers; they judge four claims instead of predicting one, so the likeliest-sounding option wins rather than the one the text licenses.

## Main idea and primary purpose  <!-- concept: central_idea -->

- **What the item really tests** — the hierarchy call: which sentence is the boss, and which work for it. A purpose item asks what job the paragraph performs, not what it is about.

- **The method**
  1. Read the whole text once; skimming loses the pivot word.
  2. Before you read the options, name the gist in five words: verb plus object, "reports a surprise result".
  3. Heading test: could the whole paragraph sit under this sentence? A claim two sentences support is a detail in a costume.
  4. On purpose items, rank by verb (note, describe, argue, refute) and match the commitment delivered.
  5. Check subject, time frame, and degree, then take the option the final sentence confirms.

- **The traps**
  - True but tiny: a fact one sentence states. Tell: you can quote that line.
  - Half-cover: right up to "however", wrong after it. Tell: it matches the opening.
  - Outside the text: true in the world, absent from the page. Tell: you must add a premise.
  - Wrong verb: "proves" where the passage suggests. Tell: it out-commits the text.

- **Timing tactic** — 35 to 45 seconds, most of it on the five-word gist. Clock biting? Read the last sentence and take the option it confirms.

- **Language to use** — "Who is the boss sentence?" "Could the whole paragraph live under that heading?" "Right words, wrong verb."

- **Worked example** — Conservators assumed visitors were the chief threat to panel paintings and fenced every gallery accordingly. Readings of airborne sulphur in three European museums suggest that ordinary dust drifting past those barriers darkens painted surfaces faster than fingertips do.
  A. Barriers meant to protect artwork can leave a quicker hazard unobstructed.
  B. Conservators had placed barriers around gallery displays.
  C. Visitors touch paintings more often than staff admit.
  D. Museums should stop displaying panel paintings.
  A is right: the paragraph exists to raise an assumption and overturn it. B is true but tiny, C needs unstated information, D prescribes without arguing.

## Selecting the detail that supports a claim  <!-- concept: supporting_detail -->

- **What the item really tests** — whether a finding bears on the exact claim the question names: same variables, same direction, nothing borrowed from your head. Truth is not the standard; bearing is.

- **The method**
  1. Split the claim: what is compared, which property is in play, which causal arrow. Note the two variables.
  2. Predict what support looks like, then what weakening looks like; half the wrong options run backwards.
  3. Three gates, in order: same variables, right direction, support delivered directly, not through a step you supply.
  4. "Therefore" test: read the option, start "therefore, about the claim...", and finish that sentence or cross it out.
  5. Prefer the option engaging the named mechanism over an analogy from a neighbouring case.

- **The traps**
  - Right topic, wrong variable: supports a different claim in the same paragraph. Tell: the "therefore" sentence stalls.
  - Direction flip: weakens quietly. Tell: buried verbs such as "fell", "only in winter".
  - Needs a bridge: true only with an unstated premise. Tell: "if we also knew that".
  - Restatement: the claim reworded, not evidenced. Tell: it answers "what", not "why".

- **Timing tactic** — 60 to 75 seconds, the heaviest items here. Short on time? Drop restatements and bridge-needing options first, since neither needs analysis, then settle the survivors against your margin notes.

- **Language to use** — "Same variables, same direction." "Am I adding a sentence?" "Does this make it likelier as written?"

- **Worked example** — A study of urban hives found colonies beside parks yielding more honey per hive than colonies beside parking lots. Researchers credit the variety of plant species available to foragers, not the number of flowering plants. The claim: variety, not abundance, explains the gap.
  A. Park and lot sites held roughly equal numbers of flowering plants per foraging area.
  B. Beekeepers near parks found their hives easier to inspect.
  C. Citywide yields per hive have risen over the past decade.
  D. Lot-side hives lost more bees to mites than park-side hives.
  A is right: with abundance equal, variety is left doing the explaining. B is another variable, C another time frame, D a rival cause that weakens.

## Logical completion of a text  <!-- concept: text_inference -->

- **What the item really tests** — strength matching. The paragraph stops one sentence short of a conclusion, and your completion must be no stronger than the evidence and add nothing new.

- **The method**
  1. Find the hinge: "therefore", "thus", "this suggests", or a "however" the ending must absorb.
  2. Inventory the premises as bare one-line facts.
  3. Draft your own ending in hedged words: "one cue may be less reliable than assumed".
  4. Strike loud words the inventory cannot carry: proves, all, never, only, the main cause, exclusively.
  5. Strike options bringing in a term, group, or period absent from the passage; a new noun means new evidence.
  6. Read the winner in place: one argument flowing.

- **The traps**
  - Strength bump: one study promoted into a demonstration. Tell: "proves" where the text gave a sample.
  - Quantifier leap: "all" from premises about some. Tell: scope wider than any premise.
  - Single cause from many factors: one of three influences crowned "the main" driver.
  - Reversed arrow: the right variables, causal direction flipped.

- **Timing tactic** — 40 to 55 seconds. Clock biting? Scan the options alone for loud words and strike two before comparing content.

- **Language to use** — "No stronger than the evidence." "Which word is doing the heavy lifting?" "Nothing new in the last line."

- **Worked example** — Entomologists have long separated cicada species by the pitch and tempo of mating songs, which differ reliably from species to species. In one region, though, two species whose songs differ sharply share nearly identical mitochondrial DNA and emerge in the same weeks each summer.
  A. Song differences here may understate how closely related the two populations are.
  B. Mating songs play no role in how females choose mates.
  C. The two are certainly one species researchers have misclassified.
  D. Emergence timing is the main factor shaping song tempo.
  A is right: the song and DNA mismatch licenses that hedge and no more. B contradicts a premise, C turns a puzzle into a verdict, D draws an unstated cause.

## Inference from a data table  <!-- concept: quant_table -->

- **What the item really tests** — whether you keep totals, rates, and shares in separate lanes while reading across and down. The errors are rarely arithmetic; they are category confusion.

- **The method**
  1. Read title, headers, and units first; say what each column counts and over what period.
  2. Identify each option's comparison: same row across columns, or same column down rows.
  3. Read direction before magnitude: endpoints, then sign, then size.
  4. Like-with-like: figures compare only when row label, period, and unit agree. Percent against counts is two sentences glued together.
  5. Ask about the base whenever a percentage appears; a bigger share of a smaller total is a smaller number.
  6. Check "twice" and "half" claims with one rough calculation.

- **The traps**
  - Level versus change: the biggest value is not the biggest increase. Tell: "grew fastest" pointing at the tallest row.
  - Cumulative misread: a running total taken as one year's increment. Tell: a header such as "to date".
  - Share versus amount: percentages drawn from two bases in one sentence.
  - Ordering swap: rows one point apart, flipped. Tell: visible only in the middle rows.

- **Timing tactic** — 60 to 90 seconds, spent on headers rather than long division. In a hurry, drop unit-mismatch options first, since they need no math, then check survivors with one subtraction.

- **Language to use** — "Shares are not amounts." "Direction first, then size." "Same row, same year, same unit?"

- **Worked example** — A table gives household electricity by source as a percent of total.
  Solar: 4 in 2010, 19 in 2022. Wind: 6 in 2010, 21 in 2022. Natural gas: 55 in 2010, 40 in 2022. Coal: 35 in 2010, 20 in 2022.
  A. Solar and wind together supplied a larger share of household electricity in 2022 than in 2010.
  B. Electricity generated from natural gas declined between 2010 and 2022.
  C. Solar's share rose by more than coal's share fell.
  D. In 2022, more households relied on solar than on coal.
  A is right: 10 combined points become 40, one column, one unit. B confuses share with output; C fails because 15 up equals 15 down; D swaps share for households.

## Inference from a graph or chart  <!-- concept: quant_graph -->

- **What the item really tests** — translating an encoding into words: which variable is on which axis, what the shape claims, what it cannot claim. Wrong answers exploit the visual instinct.

- **The method**
  1. Name both axes and units, then check where the vertical axis starts; a baseline above zero exaggerates small gaps.
  2. Let the figure type set the reading: bars compare categories, lines show change over time, scatter plots relate two variables with no time axis.
  3. Read slope, not height: does the gap between series widen, narrow, or hold? Parallel rises differ in level but not in rate.
  4. For two-variable charts read the association's direction, then refuse any option converting it into cause or time.
  5. Overlapping ranges on bars mean the difference is not yours to assert.

- **The traps**
  - Baseline illusion: a truncated axis plus an option reading "far higher". Tell: the axis starts above zero.
  - Height versus slope: the highest line has the flattest rise. Tell: a level word married to a change word.
  - Series mix-up: lines sharing a colour, differing only by marker. Tell: the claim fits the other group.
  - Correlation dressed as cause or time. Tell: a verb of producing, or of happening after.

- **Timing tactic** — 45 to 70 seconds, first ten on axes and the baseline; that kills the designed trap. In a hurry, sort options into level claims and change claims.

- **Language to use** — "Height or slope?" "Where does the axis start?" "Two variables, no cause."

- **Worked example** — A line chart plots average daily steps, in thousands, for two groups in 2016 and 2024: urban 8.2 to 8.6, rural 6.0 to 7.2. The vertical axis begins at 5.0.
  A. Urban averages stayed higher in both years while the rural average rose more.
  B. The urban average rose faster than the rural average.
  C. Rural residents overtook urban residents by 2024.
  D. The rise in steps improved participants' cardiovascular health.
  A is right: the urban level stays above while the increases compare 0.4 with 1.2, so the flatter line is the higher one. B trades slope for height, C misreads endpoints, D imports an unplotted variable.
