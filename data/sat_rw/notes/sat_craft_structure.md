# Craft and Structure

This domain asks one thing of a reader: work out what the text is actually doing — what a word means *here*, what a sentence accomplishes, how two texts answer each other, where the author stands — and reject every option that answers a different question than the one on the page. The habit that moves the score is stubbornly sequential: decide what the text means or does **before** the options think for you. Students who predict first use the choices as a checklist, and the traps lose their power.

## Words and phrases in context  <!-- concept: words_context -->

- **What the item really tests** — sense selection, not vocabulary size. The tested word carries several senses and the sentence picks exactly one. Your decision: which sense does the evidence *in this sentence* support, even if it is not the one you knew? The options are calibrated so the most familiar meaning is usually wrong.
- **The method** —
  1. Read the sentence with the word, plus one sentence on each side for the cue.
  2. Cover the options; mentally turn the tested word into a blank.
  3. Fill the blank in your own plain words, two to four of them.
  4. Match your prediction to the sentence's direction — praising, blaming, contrasting, intensifying.
  5. Ask of each option: does this fit my prediction *as this sentence uses it*, or only in the dictionary?
  6. Substitute your pick back in and reread. If the sentence now sounds confused, your pick is a trap.
- **The traps** —
  - *The familiar twin*: the word's everyday sense. Tell: it fits the passage's topic but not the sentence's claim.
  - *The temperature mistake*: right direction, wrong intensity — "obsession" where the sentence supports only "interest." Tell: the option overshoots the cue words around the blank.
  - *Right word, wrong blank*: true of the passage overall but answering a different sentence. Tell: you can defend it from paragraph two but not from the sentence itself.
  - *The grammar smuggler*: a real sense that cannot fit the syntax, a noun sense in a verb slot. Tell: the substitution reads garbled.
- **Timing tactic** — 35 to 45 seconds. When the clock bites, never compare options to each other; restate your plain-words prediction and take the closest match.
- **Language to use** — "Cover the choices, fill the blank." "Topic fit is not claim fit." "Put it back in the sentence and read."
- **Worked example** — Passage: "In 1903 a traveller described the harbour as *quiet*; by the 1950s the same docks hummed with freight cranes and night shifts." What does "quiet" most nearly mean as used?
  A. Untroubled by criticism  B. Lacking in busy activity  C. Secretly opposed to change  D. Restful despite surrounding noise
  Key: B. The semicolon contrasts 1903 with 1950s crane-and-shift bustle, so the cue is the harbour's level of activity, not mood or temperament; A and C fit the topic but not the claim, and D adds "despite noise," which the first half never grants.

## Function of a part, or the structure of the whole  <!-- concept: text_structure_purpose -->

- **What the item really tests** — the move, not the message. A part can *introduce, concede, illustrate, qualify, refute, pivot, background*. Your decision: name the job the chunk performs in the argument's machinery, or, for whole-text items, the skeleton of moves. What a part says is a summary; what it does is the answer.
- **The method** —
  1. Locate the exact part the stem asks about and say: this is a piece with a job.
  2. Read the connective tissue — "however," "for example," "indeed," "admittedly." Connectives are the author's stage directions.
  3. State the job as a one-verb phrase: "this concedes a limitation to the finding."
  4. For whole structure, chart each sentence in a few words (context, claim, evidence, qualification) and read the chain back as the shape.
  5. Judge each option against the function you named, not against whether it is true of the passage.
  6. Cross-check placement: does the described move belong here, or to a neighbouring sentence?
- **The traps** —
  - *True but irrelevant*: a faithful summary of what the part says, never naming what it does. Tell: the option has no job-verb.
  - *Right move, wrong address*: a real function performed by a *different* part. Tell: it would be correct if the stem pointed one sentence away.
  - *The inflated role*: calls background a refutation, an illustration a conclusion. Tell: the verb is one notch too powerful for the evidence.
  - *The flipped connective*: swaps concede for refute, introduce for conclude. Tell: the direction of support runs backwards.
- **Timing tactic** — 40 to 50 seconds for a part, up to 60 for whole structure. Short on time: reread only the target part and its neighbour, say the job out loud, and eliminate every option missing that verb.
- **Language to use** — "What is it doing, not what is it saying?" "Name the move in one verb." "Right move, wrong place — next option."
- **Worked example** — Passage: "Historians long held that the fort was deserted by 1820. Excavations in 2019, however, turned up datable refuse from the 1830s. The find does not settle the question of occupation, but it weakens the early date." Question: what does the underlined sentence do?
  A. It concludes that the excavation's dating methods are unreliable.  B. It qualifies the significance of the 2019 find.  C. It introduces the historians' original theory.  D. It illustrates what a typical excavation yields.
  Key: B. "Does not settle... but it weakens" is textbook qualification — it limits the evidence's force while keeping its direction; A inflates a hedge into an attack, C points at sentence one's address, and D names a move the text never makes.

## Relationship between two texts  <!-- concept: cross_text_connections -->

- **What the item really tests** — holding two stances at once and naming where they meet and split: agreement, conflict, one text extending or undercutting the other, a difference of emphasis. The decision: which statement is *fully* true of both texts as written.
- **The method** —
  1. Read Text 1 and write its stance as one clause: endorses X, because Y.
  2. Read Text 2 and do the same. Those two clauses are the comparison's raw material.
  3. Compare the clauses *before* touching the options: where do the reasons coincide, where diverge?
  4. Interrogate each option against both summaries. Any half not supported by the text kills the whole option.
  5. Name the relationship the stem asks for — agreement, conflict, or response — and make sure your pick answers *that* question.
  6. Check scope: does the option describe the texts' central stances or only a passing detail?
- **The traps** —
  - *The half-true*: accurate about Text 1 while Text 2 is silent or the opposite. Tell: only one author's view does any work in the option.
  - *The invented handshake*: claims both share a view one merely mentions or rejects. Tell: "both authors agree" with nothing in either passage that both endorse.
  - *The flipped arrow*: says Text 2 supports or answers something when the direction is reversed. Tell: "echoes" or "challenges" pointed the wrong way.
  - *The over-reader*: draws a clever implication neither text states. Tell: it rests on a syllogism you built, not a line they wrote.
- **Timing tactic** — 60 to 75 seconds, spent mostly on step 4, not re-reading. If pressed, compare your two one-clause summaries against each option's halves; you rarely need the passages again.
- **Language to use** — "One clause each, then compare." "True of both or it goes." "Same topic is not same stance."
- **Worked example** —
  Text 1: "Middle-schoolers who arrive at school at 8:30 show measurably better attendance and focus; the district should push all secondary start times later."
  Text 2: "Later start times do lift attendance, but they push athletics and after-school jobs into the dark; the gain must be weighed against those costs."
  Question: which choice best describes the relationship between the two texts?
  A. Both accept the attendance benefit, but Text 2 doubts the policy should follow without further weighing.  B. Text 1 treats attendance as unimportant while Text 2 defends it.  C. Both texts advocate moving all secondary start times later.  D. Text 2 disputes the accuracy of the attendance data cited in Text 1.
  Key: A. Text 2 explicitly concedes the attendance lift yet resists the automatic policy change, so shared finding plus split on action is true of both; B reverses Text 1, C is false of Text 2, and D attacks data Text 2 never questions.

## Point of view and how it is represented  <!-- concept: viewpoint_positioning -->

- **What the item really tests** — the author's stance toward the subject, or how a viewpoint *inside* the text (a character, a cited expert, an opponent) is framed. The core decision: is the author endorsing, hedging, merely reporting, or ironising — and whose voice speaks at that line? Description is not endorsement; quotation is not agreement.
- **The method** —
  1. Sweep for loaded words first: adjectives, adverbs, judgment-verbs. "Merely," "boldly," "reportedly," "worn smooth" — stance lives here.
  2. Ask what the author chose to emphasise; the selection and bulk of detail about one side is itself a position.
  3. Detect distance markers — "some claim," "according to" — which put a view at arm's length rather than adopt it.
  4. Place the author on a scale: enthusiastic, qualified approval, neutral report, skeptical, hostile, using at least two text words as evidence.
  5. For representation questions, ask whose viewpoint the line carries and whether the author lets it stand unchallenged, balances it, or frames it so the reader hears doubt.
  6. Defend the answer with specific words; if you cannot quote evidence, you are guessing from vibes.
- **The traps** —
  - *Endorsement by mention*: the author reports a view and the option says the author champions it. Tell: the option upgrades "notes that" to "argues for."
  - *Borrowed heat*: a quoted source's strong feelings read as the author's own. Tell: the option's intensity comes only from inside quotation marks.
  - *The temperature slip*: qualified approval called enthusiasm, neutral reporting called hostility. Tell: the adjective sits a few notches off the text's words.
  - *The missed wink*: irony taken straight, so a mocked view appears as an endorsed one. Tell: the author pairs the view with vocabulary no supporter would choose.
- **Timing tactic** — 40 to 50 seconds. Under pressure, run only the loaded-word sweep from step 1; stance questions usually resolve from three or four evaluative words without a second reading.
- **Language to use** — "Who is speaking — the author or a voice in the passage?" "Report is not endorsement." "Check the temperature of the adjectives."
- **Worked example** — Passage: "The minister called the long-delayed reform 'a change that is simply overdue,' a phrase that critics have worn smooth across three decades of commission reports." Question: what best describes the author's stance toward the minister's description?
  A. The author endorses the reform as genuinely overdue.  B. The author implies the minister's phrase has lost force through endless repetition.  C. The author charges the minister with deliberate dishonesty.  D. The author neutrally records the minister's remarks without comment.
  Key: B. "Worn smooth" is the author's own editorial image, not the minister's, signalling the phrase has been drained by overuse; A endorses past the reporting frame, C charges dishonesty the text never claims, and D misses the loaded verb entirely.
